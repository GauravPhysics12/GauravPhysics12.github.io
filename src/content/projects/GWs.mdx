On September 14, 2015, the LIGO detectors made the first direct observation of gravitational waves: the merger of two black holes roughly 1.3 billion light-years away, known as **GW150914**. This project works through that event's public data end-to-end — from raw, noisy strain data to a confirmed detection — and uses it to test General Relativity's predictions in the strong-field, highly dynamical regime where two black holes spiral into each other and merge.

In this project, I discuss:
- **Raw Strain Data:** Reading in the time-series strain data from both the Hanford (H1) and Livingston (L1) detectors around the time of the event.
- **Whitening:** Flattening the detector's frequency-dependent noise so that no single frequency band dominates the signal, making a weak astrophysical signal easier to see.
- **Bandpass Filtering:** Applying high-pass and low-pass filters to remove seismic noise at low frequencies and electronic noise at high frequencies, isolating the frequency band where the signal lives.
- **Matched Filtering:** Cross-correlating the cleaned data against a bank of theoretical waveform templates — generated from General Relativity's predictions for binary black hole mergers — to pull the signal out of the noise and measure its significance.
- **Cross-checking H1 and L1:** Confirming the same chirp signal appears in both independent detectors, offset by the light-travel time between them, which rules out a local instrumental glitch.

### The Result
The matched-filter analysis recovers the now-famous "chirp" — a signal that rises in both frequency and amplitude as the two black holes spiral together before merging — closely matching the waveform predicted by General Relativity for a binary black hole coalescence. This is precisely the kind of strong-field test that QNM ringdown analysis (see my other project) also probes, but from the *inspiral and merger* side of the signal rather than the ringdown.
